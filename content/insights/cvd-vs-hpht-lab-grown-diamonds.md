CVD and HPHT are different laboratory-grown diamond production methods, and wholesale buyers should choose between them by the finished specification, disclosure, and supply requirement rather than method alone. Both methods can produce jewellery-grade diamonds; neither method is a complete quality grade.

## Key takeaways

- CVD and HPHT are production methods, not complete quality grades.
- CVD may involve brown as-grown colour and post-growth annealing; HPHT may show flux-related or fluorescence features.
- Method, treatment, finished-stone quality, certificate, and design suitability should be reviewed together.
- For pairs and layouts, dimensional and visual matching often matters more than method preference.

## How the methods differ

CVD, or chemical vapour deposition, grows diamond layer by layer on a seed in a low-pressure vacuum chamber. HPHT, or high-pressure high-temperature growth, uses extreme pressure and heat, a carbon source, a seed, and a metal flux or catalyst.[1](https://www.gia.edu/hpht-and-cvd-diamond-growth-processes) CVD rough is commonly flat or tabular; HPHT rough has a different crystal morphology. Rough form can affect yield, shape planning, and custom-cut economics.

## Buyer-relevant comparison

| Attribute | CVD | HPHT |
|---|---|---|
| Growth environment | Low-pressure vacuum chamber with activated carbon-containing gas | High pressure and temperature with carbon, seed, and metal flux |
| Common commercial association | Low-nitrogen, Type IIa-oriented material and Surat production | Cost-competitive production and colourless or specialty output |
| Possible features to review | Brown as-grown colour, strain, layered growth, post-growth annealing | Metallic flux inclusions, zoning, fluorescence patterns, phosphorescence |
| Treatment question | Was brown colour removed or modified after growth? | Is any post-growth treatment disclosed? |
| Best buying rule | Verify the finished stone and documentation | Verify the finished stone and documentation |

## CVD: useful, but not self-explanatory

CVD can produce low-nitrogen material and is strategically important in India and Surat. GIA's technical material explains that some CVD crystals are brown as grown and may be HPHT-annealed to improve colour.[2](https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/fall-2016-observations-cvd-grown-synthetic-diamonds-review) A CVD label therefore does not tell the buyer whether the stone is untreated, annealed, Type IIa, strained, or visually suitable for a particular setting.

Type IIa is a chemical classification, not a growth method and not a complete quality grade. It can be a useful material attribute, but it does not guarantee excellent cut, high clarity, absence of treatment, or superior appearance. Ask for the report, method, treatment, measurements, colour, clarity, cut, polish, symmetry, and relevant visual evidence.

## HPHT: cost and features need context

HPHT can produce high-quality and large colourless stones, and public trade commentary has reported a lower cost base for some HPHT goods under two carats.[3](https://www.jckonline.com/editorial-article/lab-grown-wholesale-freefall/) That is directional evidence, not a universal price discount. The quote still depends on size, shape, quality, cut, treatment, certification, supply, and delivery terms.

A buyer should not reject HPHT merely because the method is unfamiliar, nor accept it merely because the price is lower. Review the actual stone for transparency, inclusions, fluorescence or phosphorescence where relevant, and the requirements of the downstream customer.

## Method matters less for pairs and layouts

For a matched pair or calibrated layout, the key issue is often dimensional and visual consistency. Two CVD stones may not match each other; two HPHT stones may not match each other. The buyer should specify measurement tolerances, outline, colour and clarity range, polish, symmetry, visual balance, and approval images. Certification identifies individual stones but does not certify the group as a pair or layout.[4](https://www.igi.org/reports/lab-grown-diamond-report/)

For custom cuts, discuss rough feasibility before promising a shape, size, yield, or price. CVD and HPHT morphologies can lead to different cutting plans. The best method is the one that can deliver the finished design with the required disclosure and repeatability.

## Certification and disclosure

IGI is commonly used for wholesale comparison and can record laboratory-grown origin, core 4Cs information, and, where requested, growth method and treatment comments.[4](https://www.igi.org/reports/lab-grown-diamond-report/) GIA may be requested when a channel needs its institutional name, although its colourless laboratory-grown service uses Premium or Standard assessments.[5](https://www.gia.edu/gem-lab-service/laboratory-grown-diamond) GCAL or GCAL 8X may be relevant for performance-led goods where optical symmetry, imaging, and cut evidence are part of the product proposition.[6](https://www.gcalusa.com/gcal-8x-cut-grade.html)

The practical procurement rule is simple: require method and treatment disclosure, then judge the finished stone against the design and commercial brief. Do not use CVD or HPHT as a shortcut for quality. For a recurring programme, also record the accepted method, treatment language, report format, and inspection criteria so a later shipment can be compared on the same basis. That record is more useful than a general preference for one production route.
