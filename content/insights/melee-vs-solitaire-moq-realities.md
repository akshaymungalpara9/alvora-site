Minimum order quantity is not a sales tactic; it is a reflection of how each category of diamond is actually produced. Understanding why a melee parcel has one kind of minimum while a solitaire has another — and why bespoke work has a third — will help you structure first orders that fit both the manufacturer's process and your own cash flow.

## Key takeaways

- Melee is a batch product: cutting, sorting and quality control all happen on parcels, not individual stones.
- Solitaires are inventory products: singles are possible because the stone already exists and is graded.
- Bespoke custom-cut minimums reflect set-up and programming time, not the value of the stones.
- Consolidating sizes and specifications across designs reduces the number of parcels you must buy.
- Exact minimums vary by product — the figures live on the [melee diamonds](/melee-diamonds), [calibrated diamond layouts](/calibrated-diamond-layouts) and [custom-cut diamonds](/custom-cut-diamonds) pages, not in this article.

## Why melee is ordered in bulk

Melee stones — the small diamonds used for pavé, halos, channel settings and accents — are manufactured in volume. Rough is grown in batches, sawn and cleaved in batches, polished on automated or semi-automated lines, and then sieved and sorted into size and quality parcels. Every step of that chain is calibrated for hundreds or thousands of stones at once; the per-stone handling cost of a five-piece melee order would exceed the value of the stones themselves. There is also a demand-side logic: trade reporting has repeatedly noted how lab-grown melee demand surged as retailers built entirely lab-grown pieces, tightening supply in small sizes even as one-carat prices fell [1](https://rapaport.com/magazine-article/the-economics-of-lab-grown/).

The practical consequence for buyers: plan melee purchases around collections, not single jobs. If three of your designs use 1.0–1.1 mm rounds, order one consolidated parcel and allocate it across the range.

## Why solitaires can be ordered as singles

A certified solitaire is a finished, graded, individually documented item sitting in inventory. Selling one of them to you involves picking, checking and dispatching — no production set-up, no batch economics. That is why single-stone ordering is normal in the solitaire category. What solitaires do demand, instead of volume, is precision of specification: shape, exact carat or millimetre size, colour, clarity, cut quality and certificate all stated and matched to a specific stone. Single-stone buying rewards buyers who know exactly what they want; bulk buying rewards buyers who know exactly what they will use.

## Where bespoke production sits in between

Bespoke custom-cut work — a proprietary shape, a non-standard calibrated run, a matched layout for a specific design — carries higher minimums than either category above, for a simple reason: the manufacturer's fixed costs are the same whether the run yields five stones or five hundred. Programming, tooling, first-article inspection and process adjustment all happen before the first acceptable stone exists. Once the run is set up, per-stone costs fall steeply, which is why manufacturers price bespoke work to absorb that set-up across a reasonable quantity. Bespoke production also carries longer lead times — at Alvora Diamonds, bespoke custom-cut or calibrated-layout work typically takes around 7–10 working days, against 2–3 working days for stock and standard orders.

## How to plan your first order across all three

| Category | Order logic | Best first step |
|---|---|---|
| Melee parcels | Consolidate sizes across your collection | One parcel covering 2–3 designs |
| Solitaires | Order to exact specification, one stone at a time | A single certified stone to test the supplier |
| Bespoke custom-cut | Commit set-up cost only with confirmed design demand | A sample run before full production |

## A note on "no minimum" marketing

You will occasionally see suppliers advertise "no minimum order." Read this carefully rather than celebrating it. For solitaires and stock lines, no minimum can be entirely genuine, because the category supports singles. For melee and bespoke work, "no minimum" usually means one of three things: the goods are being pulled from another customer's parcel (with consequences for matching), the price per stone has absorbed the batch economics (in which case compare carefully against parcel pricing), or the supplier is a trader rather than a manufacturer and is repacking someone else's production. None of these is automatically disqualifying, but each should change how you verify quality and consistency. A manufacturer that explains its minimums honestly is telling you something useful about its process; one that promises everything is telling you something useful about its sales department.

## In closing

Minimum order quantities become far less frustrating once you see them as production physics rather than negotiation positions. The useful question is not "how few can I buy?" but "which category does this design actually need?" Melee for the collection, singles for the centrepiece, bespoke only when the design genuinely requires it — that framing keeps inventory lean, set-up costs justified and relationships with your manufacturer straightforward.
